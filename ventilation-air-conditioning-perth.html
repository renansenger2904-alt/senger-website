02The system

A kitchen exhaust is a system, not a fan.

Most problems on site come from designing one part at a time. I design the whole loop so the numbers agree with each other.

  1. Roof discharge. Vertical, at velocity, and clear of air intakes, openings and boundaries, so neighbours and your own supply fan don't breathe it.
  2. Exhaust fan and grease duct. Duty set from the real duct route: straight runs, bends, filters and the exit, not a guess off a catalogue.
  3. Canopy. Sized to the appliances under it, their type and rating, with the overhang and mounting height the Standard asks for.
  4. Make-up air. Every litre extracted has to come back in. Too little and doors whistle, burners starve and the hood stops capturing.
  5. Room balance and comfort. Supply placed so it doesn't blow across the hood, and a check on heat, because cooking lines and refrigeration condensers heat the room fast.
  6. Interlocks. No airflow, no gas. The control logic between fans, gas valve and reset is written down, so the electrician and gasfitter wire the same thing.

04Inside a real package

What “checked” actually means.

Every firm says its work is checked. Here is what that word bought on a recent bakery fit‑out in the Perth metro area, issued for pricing. Client, site and equipment brands withheld.

521 rows

Every number calculated twice, by two different methods

Airflow, duct velocity, fan pressure, gas ventilation, noise, support loads. Each quantity was worked a second time from first principles, with its own friction‑factor iteration, so the check could not inherit the first calculation’s mistake. The two were compared line by line: 521 rows, zero differences.

32% more air

Two editions of the same standard disagreed by 32%

The current AS 1668.2 gave 934 L/s for that canopy. The older edition named by the local government food guideline gave 1229 L/s for the identical canopy. A fan selected on the lower number would have been signed off, installed, and then failed the council check. The higher figure was adopted, rounded to 1250 L/s, with both calculations printed side by side so the choice is auditable.

240 N

A fire‑mode assumption that would have trapped people

The obvious fire logic is to stop the supply fan. Run the exhaust without make‑up air, on a tight envelope with the make‑up air path shut, and the room goes to roughly 240 Pa negative. That is about 240 N at the handle of a 920 mm leaf, before any door closer. More than double the 110 N door opening force AS 1668.1 sets for pressurised stair doors, on the doors people leave through. The design keeps both fans running and a hold point blocks construction until the building’s fire strategy confirms it.

0.48 m

The cheap idea, killed with measurements instead of opinion

Reusing the old riser as the fresh‑air intake would have saved an entire roof penetration. Six metres was the minimum separation. It was sized, checked, and rejected: the closest discharge came out at 0.48 m, the furthest at 4.12 m, and the discharges could not move because the appliances fix them. The reasoning is written down, so nobody proposes it again in six months.

07Before you call

The six questions I get every time.

Straight answers, including the ones that might talk you out of hiring me.

Can I reuse the canopy and ducts the last tenant left behind?

Often yes, and it is usually the single biggest saving on the job. But it is a check, not an assumption. What matters is whether the canopy still suits your appliances, whether the filter bank geometry meets the current standard, whether the duct route and riser can carry the airflow your equipment now needs, and whether the roof discharge still clears every air intake. On a recent job the existing canopy was kept, but its filter bank sat outside the prescriptive angle limit, so it needed a documented equivalence case rather than a shrug.

What to check before you reuse a kitchen exhaust in WA

What is AS 1668.2, and why does everyone keep saying it?

It is the Australian standard for mechanical ventilation. It sets how much air a canopy has to pull for your particular appliances, how ducts and discharges have to be arranged, and how far a discharge must sit from any fresh‑air intake. Your building surveyor and your council environmental health officer will expect the design to meet it. Alongside it sits AS/NZS 5601.1, the gas standard, which governs combustion air and the interlock that stops the burners if the ventilation fails.

The catch is that local guidelines sometimes name an older edition of AS 1668.2 than the one currently published, and the two can give materially different airflows for the same kitchen. That is worth knowing before a fan is ordered.

Do I need an engineer, or can my mechanical contractor sort it out?

Plenty of straightforward kitchens get built well by a good mechanical contractor. Where a design earns its keep is when the answer is contested: a council asking which edition of the standard you used, a gas inspector asking how the combustion air figure was reached, an existing canopy you want to keep, a tight roof where discharges and intakes are fighting for space, or two contractors returning prices that are thousands apart because they each guessed at a different scope.

To be straight with you about credentials: I am a member of Engineers Australia (MIEAust). Western Australia’s registration scheme for building engineers does not take full effect until 1 July 2027. If your project specifically requires a Chartered or registered engineer’s signature today, say so early and I will tell you plainly whether I am the right fit.

Do you supply or install the canopy and fans?

No. I design, you build. I do not sell equipment, so there is no margin riding on which fan gets specified, and no reason to size anything larger than the calculation says. The package is written so your contractor, or three competing contractors, can price the same scope from the same drawings. Installation, gasfitting and commissioning stay with the licensed trades.

How long does it take?

A preliminary concept, enough to size the fans, fix the roof penetrations and unblock the builder, usually comes back in days from your equipment list and floor plan. The full package follows once the appliance data sheets are in and I have measured the site. The honest variable is not my drawing time, it is how quickly the equipment supplier sends real airflow figures and how quickly decisions get made on reuse and canopy height.

Fixed fee, agreed in writing before anything starts, after a free scoping call. I do not publish a rate card because the honest range is wide. A reuse check on one canopy and a full kitchen with multiple gas appliances, a gas interlock and a congested roof are different jobs.
