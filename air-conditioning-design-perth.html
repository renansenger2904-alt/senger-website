02The kitchen case

Cooling a kitchen is an air balance problem first.

A production kitchen can throw tens of kilowatts into the room from ovens, fryers and the condensers of every fridge and freezer in it. The exhaust hoods take some of that away, and the make-up air that replaces the extracted air arrives at whatever the outside is doing, which in a Perth summer is not cool. Adding a bigger air conditioner to that is the expensive way to lose.

The order that works: capture the heat where it is made, hoods sized and positioned properly; get condenser heat out of the space, remote or water cooled; then decide what the make-up air needs, untempered, evaporatively cooled or refrigerated; and only then size the cooling for what is left. That sequence usually ends with smaller equipment than the first quote assumed.

If the exhaust is part of the same job, the two designs are done together so the airflows agree. See kitchen exhaust and ventilation design.

04Questions

What owners and builders ask.

What is a heat load calculation and why does it matter?

It adds up everything that heats or cools the space: outdoor conditions, building fabric, glazing and orientation, people, lighting, equipment such as ovens, fryers and refrigeration condensers, and the outdoor air the ventilation brings in. The total sets the capacity of the equipment. Guessing it usually means either an undersized system that never holds temperature, or an oversized one that short cycles, costs more to run and dehumidifies poorly.

Can air conditioning fix a hot commercial kitchen?

Only after the heat is dealt with at the source. Exhaust hoods remove part of it, and large untempered make-up air on a hot Perth day brings heat in. The usual order is: capture heat at the appliances, move refrigeration condensers out of the space or water cool them, then temper the make-up air and size the cooling for what is left.

Do you install or service air conditioning?

No. This is design and documentation only: loads, selections, layouts and the written basis. The mechanical contractor supplies, installs, commissions and certifies the system, and refrigerant work is done by the appropriately licensed trades.

What do you need from me to quote?

A floor plan, the use of each space and its occupancy, an equipment list with kW ratings, ceiling heights, photos of the roof or plant area, and any existing equipment you want to keep. That is enough for a fixed-fee proposal.
